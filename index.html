Physiology & terminology

Every value we report is a subdivision of one thing: the volume of gas the lungs can hold, and how fast it moves in and out. Learn the map first and the rest of the hub becomes naming.

The lung subdivisions

Four volumes that do not overlap, and four capacities that are sums of volumes. Spirometry measures everything except the residual volume; anything containing RV must come from a lung volume test.

The one relationship that explains most reports

Obstruction empties slowly, so it traps gas: RV and FRC rise, TLC may rise, and FEV1/FVC falls. Restriction is a small lung: TLC falls, and because everything shrinks together the FEV1/FVC ratio is normal or high. If you remember only this, you can sort most reports before reading a single z-score.

Forced expiratory indices

  • FVC — forced vital capacity. Volume exhaled with maximal force from TLC to end of forced expiration.
  • FEV1 — volume exhaled in the first second, timed from the back-extrapolated start point.
  • FEV1/FVC — the ratio that defines obstruction. Report as a decimal or percentage, but interpret against its own z-score, not a fixed 0.70.
  • PEF — peak expiratory flow. Highly effort dependent, reached within about 120 ms of a good blast. A rounded peak means poor effort or a slow start.
  • FEF25–75 — mean flow over the middle half of the FVC. Effort independent but very variable; never diagnose "small airway disease" on this alone.
  • FET — forced expiratory time. Useful as a quality marker.
  • BEV — back-extrapolated volume. The volume "lost" to a hesitant start, and the first thing to check on any effort.

BTPS, ATPS and STPD

Gas volume changes with temperature, pressure and humidity, so we must state the conditions under which a volume is expressed. The analyser applies these corrections, but you have to enter the ambient conditions correctly for them to work.

ConditionMeaningUsed for

A daily habit worth keeping

Enter ambient temperature, barometric pressure and humidity at the start of every session and whenever the room conditions change. A wrong temperature quietly biases every volume in that session, and nothing on the report will look obviously wrong.

Reference equations and z-scores

A measured value on its own means nothing. It is compared with what a healthy person of the same age, height and sex would produce, using a reference equation. The current standard is the Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) set.

  • GLI 2012 for spirometry (ages 3–95), GLI 2017 for DLCO, GLI 2021 for static lung volumes.
  • The GLI Global spirometry equation is race-neutral and is what the 2022 interpretation standard recommends. Race-specific equations are no longer advised.
  • Whatever set your lab uses, it must be stated on the report, and it must not be changed mid-study for a patient being followed over time.

A z-score says how many standard deviations a result sits from the predicted mean for that person. It removes the age, height and sex bias that percent predicted carries, especially at the extremes of age.

The number to memorise

The lower limit of normal (LLN) is the 5th percentile, which is a z-score of −1.645. Anything below that is outside the normal range; 5 % of healthy people will fall there by definition.

Acceptable, usable, repeatable

Three words with precise meanings that come up on every test page.

  • Acceptable — the manoeuvre met every criterion, start to finish. All reported values may be taken from it.
  • Usable — the manoeuvre failed a criterion that affects only part of it. A blow with a good start but early termination is usable for FEV1 but not for FVC.
  • Repeatable — two or more acceptable manoeuvres agree within a defined tolerance. Acceptability is about one effort; repeatability is about the session.

Patient preparation & safety

Most poor-quality studies are decided before the patient sits down. Preparation, contraindication screening and infection control are as much a part of the test as the manoeuvre.

Before the appointment

Give these instructions when booking, and confirm them at reception. Record any deviation on the report — an un-withheld bronchodilator changes how the physician reads the study.

  • No smoking or vaping for at least 1 hour before testing; for DLCO, ideally 24 hours, and record carboxyhaemoglobin if available.
  • No vigorous exercise for 1 hour before.
  • No large meal for 2 hours before. No alcohol for 4 hours (8 hours for DLCO and challenge testing).
  • Loose clothing that does not restrict the chest or abdomen.
  • Bring dentures if well fitting — they help the lip seal. Remove them only if loose.
  • Bring the inhaler list, and note the time of the last dose of every inhaled drug.

Bronchodilator withholding times

Do not withhold silently

Never instruct a patient to withhold a maintenance medication without the referring team's agreement, and never withhold rescue medication from a symptomatic patient. If the patient arrives having taken a dose, test them anyway, document the drug and the time, and let the physician decide.

Relative contraindications to forced manoeuvres

The 2019 standard frames these as risks to be weighed by the referring physician, not absolute bans. Forced expiration raises intrathoracic, intracranial, intraocular and intra-abdominal pressure, and provokes cough and syncope. Escalate rather than decide alone.

Cardiovascular

Myocardial infarction within 1 week · systemic hypotension or severe hypertension · significant atrial or ventricular arrhythmia · decompensated heart failure · uncontrolled pulmonary hypertension · acute cor pulmonale · clinically unstable pulmonary embolism · history of syncope related to forced exhalation or cough.

Raised pressure

Cerebral aneurysm · brain surgery within 4 weeks · recent concussion with ongoing symptoms · eye surgery within 1 week · thoracic, abdominal or cranial surgery within 4 weeks · late-term pregnancy complications.

Airway & chest

Pneumothorax · large bullae on imaging · haemoptysis of unknown origin · recent thoracic surgery · unstable or severe uncontrolled asthma.

Infection control

Active or suspected transmissible infection — pulmonary tuberculosis, and respiratory infection managed under droplet or airborne precautions. Test at the end of the list in a room with adequate air exchange, or defer.

Measuring the patient correctly

Predicted values depend on height more than anything else. A 2 cm error moves the predicted FVC by roughly 2 %, which is enough to change a borderline result.

  • Measure standing height with a stadiometer, shoes off, heels together, looking straight ahead. Do not accept a reported height.
  • If the patient cannot stand straight — kyphoscoliosis, contracture, amputation — use arm span and document the substitution. Arm span divided by 1.06 approximates height in adults; state the method on the report.
  • Record age to the nearest year on the day of testing, and weight for DLCO and exercise testing.
  • Record the ethnicity field per your local policy, and note which reference equation the software applied.

Infection control at the mouthpiece

  • Use an in-line bacterial/viral filter for every patient on every device where the standard allows one. Filters must be validated for the device so that resistance and dead space are accounted for.
  • Single-use mouthpieces and nose clips. Never reuse a nose clip between patients.
  • Hand hygiene before and after every patient contact, and after handling used circuits.
  • Clean and disinfect all contact surfaces between patients. For the plethysmograph, wipe the interior and allow the cabin to ventilate with the door open between patients.
  • Handle used circuits as contaminated: gloves on, remove the filter into clinical waste, then hand hygiene.

Calibration & quality control

Quality control is what separates a diagnostic laboratory from a device in a room. A result you cannot defend with a calibration log is not a result.

Standards
ATS/ERS Spirometry 2019 · ERS/ATS Lung Volumes 2023 · ERS/ATS DLCO 2017
Owner
The technologist opening the lab that day, countersigned by the section head weekly
Records
Retained and available for JCI / CBAHI review. Log every check, including failures and the corrective action

The distinction that gets confused

Calibration adjusts the device so its output matches a known input. Calibration verification checks that the device still reads a known input correctly, without changing anything. Most modern systems are verified daily and calibrated only when verification fails or after service.

Daily — before the first patient

  1. Record ambient conditionsTemperature, barometric pressure, relative humidity. Re-enter whenever they change materially during the day.
  2. Volume verification with a 3-L syringeThe reading must fall within ±3 % of 3.000 L. Allowing 0.5 % syringe error, the acceptable window is 2.925–3.075 L.
  3. Flow linearityDeliver the 3 L at three different speeds — slow, medium and fast — spanning roughly 0.5 to 12 L·s⁻¹. Each must meet the same ±3 % window. A device that passes slowly and fails fast has a sensor problem.
  4. Gas analyser zero and checkFor DLCO and CPET systems, zero the analysers and run the manufacturer's gas check. Confirm test gas cylinder contents and expiry.
  5. PlethysmographBox calibration per manufacturer, door seal check, and confirm the cabin returns to a stable baseline.
Syringe technique matters

Hold the syringe level with the device, use a smooth full stroke without stopping, and keep your hands off the barrel — body heat expands the gas. Store the syringe in the testing room so it is at room temperature, and have it checked against a reference at least annually.

Weekly

  • Leak check. Apply a constant pressure of at least 3 cmH2O with the outlet occluded and confirm volume loss stays under 30 mL over one minute.
  • DLCO simulation. Run a DLCO manoeuvre using the 3-L syringe in patient mode. The result should be at or near zero, and the calculated VA should match the syringe volume plus system dead space. This is the single best check that the gas analysers, timing and volume signal all agree.
  • Isothermal volume check for the plethysmograph, using the isothermal bottle.

Biological quality control

Syringes test the hardware. Biological controls test the whole system — hardware, software, reference equations and technique — the way a patient experiences it.

  1. Recruit two or three healthy non-smoking staff membersThey should be available long term. Record their baseline properly.
  2. Establish the baselineAt least ten sessions over two or three weeks, then calculate the mean and standard deviation for each index.
  3. Test at a fixed intervalWeekly to monthly for spirometry, and at least quarterly for DLCO. Same subject, same device, same time of day.
  4. Apply the limitA result outside ±2 SD of that subject's own mean, or a run of results drifting the same way, means investigate. For DLCO, a common working limit is within 10 % or 3 units of the established mean.
  5. Act on a failureRepeat the check. If it fails again, quarantine the device, review calibration and technique, and escalate. Do not report patient studies from a device that failed biological control until the cause is found.

When a check fails

Stop testing on that device. Repeat the check to rule out technique. Inspect for an obvious cause — loose tubing, damaged sensor, wrong ambient entry, expired gas. Recalibrate if the system permits, then re-verify. If it still fails, tag the device out of service and notify the section head. Document the failure, the action taken, and whether any patient studies since the last passing check need review.

Core lung function

Spirometry

The most requested and most coach-dependent test in the lab. Everything downstream — bronchodilator response, lung volumes, DLCO interpretation — depends on getting this right.

What it measures

The patient inflates to total lung capacity, then empties as fast and as completely as possible. The rate of emptying is set by airway calibre and by lung elastic recoil; the volume emptied is set by lung size and by how much gas is trapped behind closing airways.

Immediately after the blast begins, the intrathoracic airways are compressed by pleural pressure and flow becomes effort independent — pushing harder does not increase flow beyond that point. This is why PEF depends on how hard the patient blasts, while the descending limb and FEF25–75 do not. It is also why a submaximal effort produces a curve that looks smooth but reports a falsely low FEV1.

Procedure

  1. Prepare and explainCheck contraindications and withholding. Enter height, age, sex and ambient conditions. Demonstrate the manoeuvre yourself — a live demonstration beats any explanation.
  2. PositionSeated, back straight, nose clip on, chin slightly up. Loosen tight clothing. Instruct the patient to seal the lips fully around the mouthpiece with the tongue underneath, not blocking it.
  3. Tidal breathingTwo or three quiet breaths to settle and to confirm a good seal. "Just breathe normally for me."
  4. Inspire fully to TLCA rapid, complete inspiration. Pause at the top for less than two seconds. "Now take the biggest breath you can — all the way in, more, more, keep going."
  5. Blast outMaximal force from the very first instant, with no hesitation. "BLAST it out — hard, fast!"
  6. Keep goingContinue until the flow plateaus or the patient genuinely cannot continue. "Keep going, keep going, squeeze it all out, don't stop."
  7. Inspire back to TLCA maximal inspiration at the end closes the loop and confirms the patient started from full inflation. "And a big breath back in, fast, all the way."
  8. Review before repeatingLook at the trace, not the numbers. Identify the specific fault and give one correction before the next attempt. Rest at least 60 seconds between efforts.
Coaching is the skill

Match your energy to what you want from the patient. Stand up, use your hands, raise your voice for the blast and drop it for the sustained phase. Correct one thing at a time — a patient told three things at once will do none of them. If the patient is failing the same way three times, change the instruction, not the volume of your voice.

Acceptability criteria

Start of test

  • Back-extrapolated volume (BEV) ≤ 5 % of FVC or 0.100 L, whichever is greater. A hesitant start invalidates FEV1.
  • A sharp, rapid rise to PEF, with no plateau or rounding at the peak.

During the manoeuvre — none of these may occur

  • Cough in the first second of exhalation, or any cough that affects the measurement.
  • Glottis closure, which shows as an abrupt drop of flow to zero.
  • Early termination or an abrupt end of expiration.
  • Obstructed mouthpiece — tongue, teeth, or a finger over the sensor.
  • An extra breath taken during the manoeuvre.
  • Leak around the mouthpiece.
  • Evidence that the patient did not start from full inflation, or did not finish at full inflation on the closing inspiration.

End of forced expiration — at least one must be met

  • A plateau of at least 1 second with less than 0.025 L expired.
  • A forced expiratory time of 15 seconds or more.
  • The patient cannot or should not continue — recorded as such, and the effort may still be usable for FEV1.

Grades C to F are still reported. Never delete efforts to make a session look better, and never report a session at a grade it did not earn. Which values to report

  • Report the largest FVC and the largest FEV1 from all acceptable manoeuvres, even if they come from different blows.
  • Report FEV1/FVC calculated from those two largest values, as the standard directs.
  • Report other indices such as FEF25–75 from the single acceptable manoeuvre with the largest sum of FVC + FEV1.
  • Include the flow–volume and volume–time traces of the reported efforts on the report.

Slow vital capacity

An SVC or IVC is performed unforced, from TLC to RV or from RV to TLC, and is used to derive lung volume subdivisions. In airway obstruction the SVC is often larger than the FVC because slow emptying avoids dynamic airway compression; that difference is itself a sign of gas trapping. The two largest acceptable VC values should agree within 0.150 L.

Core lung function

Bronchodilator responsiveness

The definition changed in 2022 and this is the most common source of out-of-date reporting in a PFT lab. Learn the new formula and stop quoting 12 % and 200 mL.

Standard
ERS/ATS Technical Standard on Interpretive Strategies for Routine Lung Function Tests, 2022 — Eur Respir J 2022;60:2101499
Measures
Change in FEV1 and FVC after a short-acting bronchodilator
Duration
Roughly 30 minutes including the waiting period
Term
"Bronchodilator responsiveness", not "reversibility" — reversibility implies the obstruction was abolished

The current definition

BDR is present when

(post − pre) ÷ predicted × 100  >  10 %

… for either FEV1 or FVC, in litres, using the same GLI predicted value that appears on the report.

Expressing the change against the predicted value rather than the patient's own baseline removes the bias that made small people and people with severe disease appear more responsive than they were. The 10 % threshold approximates the 95th percentile of change seen in health.

Worked example

A 50-year-old man, 170 cm. Pre-bronchodilator FEV1 2.00 L, post 2.40 L, predicted 3.32 L.

  • By the 2022 standard: (2.40 − 2.00) ÷ 3.32 × 100 = 12.0 % → responsive.
  • By the old 2005 rule: 0.40 L is 20 % of baseline and exceeds 200 mL → also positive, but the two rules disagree often enough that you must use only the current one.

Procedure

  1. Baseline spirometryAchieve an acceptable, repeatable pre-bronchodilator study first. There is no point testing a response against a bad baseline.
  2. Administer the bronchodilatorSalbutamol 400 µg as four separate 100 µg actuations through a spacer, roughly 30 seconds apart, each followed by a breath-hold of about 10 seconds. Coach the inhaler technique — a poorly delivered dose reads as a negative test.
  3. Wait10–15 minutes for salbutamol. If ipratropium 160 µg is used instead, wait 30 minutes.
  4. Repeat spirometrySame manoeuvre, same acceptability and repeatability criteria. Post-bronchodilator efforts are graded exactly like pre.
  5. Calculate and recordReport pre and post values, the absolute and percent change, and the change as a percentage of predicted. State the drug, the dose, the delivery device and the waiting time.
Interpretation caveats worth saying out loud
  • An absent response does not exclude asthma and does not mean the patient will not benefit from a bronchodilator.
  • A present response does not by itself diagnose asthma; it occurs in COPD too.
  • A response in FVC alone, with little FEV1 change, suggests reduced gas trapping rather than changed airway calibre. It is a real and clinically useful finding.
  • Airflow obstruction is defined on the post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC.

Sources: Stanojevic S et al. ERS/ATS technical standard on interpretive strategies for routine lung function tests. Eur Respir J 2022;60:2101499 · ATS/ERS Spirometry 2019 Update.

Core lung function

Static lung volumes

The only way to confirm restriction, and the only way to quantify gas trapping. Substantially revised in 2023 — linked spirometry is now mandatory and FRC values are averaged rather than picked.

Standard
ERS/ATS Technical Statement: Standardisation of the Measurement of Lung Volumes, 2023 Update — Eur Respir J 2023;62:2201519
Measures
FRC, and from it RV, TLC, plus IC, ERV and VC from the linked spirometry
Methods
Body plethysmography (reference method) · multiple-breath washout · helium dilution
Duration
10–15 min for plethysmography; 20–30 min for gas dilution with washout intervals
Key change 2023

Plethysmography — the principle

Boyle's law: at constant temperature, pressure multiplied by volume is constant. The patient sits in a sealed cabin. At end-expiration a shutter closes the mouthpiece, and the patient pants gently against the closed shutter. Because no gas leaves the chest, compressing and decompressing the thoracic gas changes both mouth pressure and cabin pressure, and the ratio of those changes gives the volume of gas in the thorax.

The critical property: plethysmography measures all intrathoracic gas, including gas behind completely closed airways and gas in bullae. Gas dilution measures only the gas that communicates with the airway opening. In severe obstruction, FRCpleth exceeds FRCHe, and the difference estimates the trapped, non-communicating volume.

Plethysmography procedure

  1. Seat and sealPatient upright in the cabin, feet flat, hands supporting the cheeks and floor of mouth, nose clip on. Close the door and allow thermal equilibration — usually 30–60 seconds until the baseline is stable.
  2. Stable tidal breathingRecord at least three to five tidal breaths at a consistent end-expiratory level. Stability means the difference between the highest and lowest end-expiratory lung volume is within 15 % of the tidal volume.
  3. Shutter closes at FRCClose at or near the end-expiratory level. Drift between the true FRC level and the occlusion level — the switch-in error — should be under 200 mL.
  4. PantGentle panting for 2–3 seconds at 0.5–1.0 Hz (30–60 per minute), with mouth pressure swings around ±10 cmH2O. "Small quick panting breaths, like a little dog — keep your cheeks firm." Above 1.5 Hz the signal distorts; below 0.5 Hz the controlled leak starts to bleed pressure away.
  5. Shutter opens — linked IC, then linked SVCImmediately after release, an inspiratory capacity to TLC, then a slow full expiration to RV, without coming off the mouthpiece. "Now all the way in … and slowly all the way out, keep going, empty right down."
  6. RepeatThree to five technically satisfactory panting manoeuvres.

Acceptability & repeatability

  • Tidal breathing stable before shutter closure, with a consistent end-expiratory level.
  • Panting loops closed, consistent in shape, free of artefact and with minimal hysteresis.
  • Tangent line correctly placed on the loop — you must review this and correct it only if the software has clearly placed it wrongly.
  • Panting frequency within 0.5–1.0 Hz; switch-in error under 200 mL.
  • Linked IC and SVC performed in the same manoeuvre, with the SVC meeting the usual VC acceptability including a plateau.

Repeatability is calculated as (largest FRC − smallest FRC) ÷ mean FRC. Sessions are graded A to F, with grades D, E and U reported but interpreted with caution.

Two things the 2023 update changed in daily practice
  • Average, don't pick. Where two or more manoeuvres meet the repeatability criterion, average the FRC values and their associated linked spirometry rather than reporting the single best one.
  • Report TLC as RV plus the largest acceptable inspiratory VC. Airway resistance measurement must not be performed during the same panting manoeuvre used for FRC.

Gas dilution methods

Helium dilution and multiple-breath washout measure only communicating gas. They are useful when a cabin is unavailable or the patient cannot tolerate one — claustrophobia, wheelchair, obesity beyond the cabin's limits.

  • Helium dilution is a closed circuit. Equilibrium is reached when the helium concentration changes by less than 0.02 % over 30 seconds. Premature termination underestimates FRC, RV and TLC.
  • Multiple-breath washout is an open circuit; the tracer gas is washed out until the end-tidal concentration falls below the defined end point. The 2023 update generalises the technique beyond nitrogen to other tracer gases.
  • Allow a recovery interval of about 1.5 times the wash-in or washout time — commonly 10–15 minutes — before repeating, so residual tracer does not corrupt the next measurement.
  • Any leak at the mouth or nose invalidates the test completely. Check the seal continuously.

Core lung function

DLCO — single-breath carbon monoxide uptake

The one routine test that looks past mechanics at the gas exchange surface itself. Also the test most sensitive to sloppy technique, because three separate signals — volume, timing and gas concentration — all have to be right at once.

Standard
2017 ERS/ATS Standards for Single-Breath Carbon Monoxide Uptake in the Lung — Eur Respir J 2017;49:1600016
Measures
DLCO (TLCO), alveolar volume VA, and the transfer coefficient KCO = DLCO/VA
Units
mL·min−1·mmHg−1 (ATS) or mmol·min−1·kPa−1 (ERS, SI). 1 mmol·min−1·kPa−1 ≈ 2.987 mL·min−1·mmHg−1
Duration
15–25 min, driven by the mandatory interval between manoeuvres
Manoeuvres
2 acceptable and repeatable; no more than 5 attempts in a session

Why carbon monoxide

CO binds to haemoglobin roughly 200 times more avidly than oxygen, so as soon as a CO molecule crosses the alveolar membrane it is bound and effectively removed from the capillary plasma. The partial pressure of CO in the blood therefore stays near zero throughout the breath-hold, and the driving pressure across the membrane is simply the alveolar CO pressure. Uptake becomes limited by diffusion rather than by perfusion, which is exactly what we want to measure.

An inert tracer gas — helium or methane — is inhaled at the same time. It does not cross the membrane, so its dilution tells you the alveolar volume the CO was distributed into, and lets you calculate how much of the CO disappearance was genuine uptake.

Procedure

  1. PrepareConfirm no smoking that day, no supplemental oxygen for the preceding period if it is safe to remove it, and no alcohol. Record haemoglobin, and carboxyhaemoglobin if available. Enter height, weight, age, sex and ambient conditions.
  2. Settle on the mouthpieceNose clip on, seated, a few tidal breaths until the pattern is regular.
  3. Exhale unforced to RVComplete but not forced, in under about 6 seconds. "Breathe all the way out, steadily, right down to empty."
  4. Rapid full inspiration of the test gasMust be fast — at least 85 % of the inspired volume within 4 seconds. Inspired volume must reach at least 90 % of the largest previously recorded VC, or at least 85 % with VA agreeing within 200 mL or 5 % of the other acceptable manoeuvres. "Now snap the biggest breath in you can, fast, and hold it."
  5. Breath-hold10 seconds, acceptable range 8–12 s, timed by the Jones–Meade method. Hold with a relaxed, open glottis — no Valsalva pushing against a closed throat, no Müller manoeuvre sucking against it. "Relax and hold — don't push, don't pull."
  6. Exhale smoothly and completelyIn under 4 seconds. The system discards a washout volume of 0.75–1.00 L (reduced to 0.50 L when VC is under 2 L) and then collects an alveolar sample of 0.5–1.0 L. "And blow out steadily all the way, don't stop early."
  7. Wait before repeatingAt least 4 minutes, and up to 10 minutes in significant obstruction, so that CO and tracer gas wash out. Sitting the patient upright and having them breathe room air speeds this up.

Acceptability & repeatability

  • Inspired volume meets the VI criterion above, achieved rapidly.
  • Breath-hold time between 8 and 12 seconds, at a stable lung volume.
  • No leak, no Valsalva or Müller manoeuvre, no cough during the hold.
  • Washout and sample volumes within specification, sample collected in under 4 seconds.
  • Two acceptable values agreeing within 2 mL·min−1·mmHg−1 (0.67 mmol·min−1·kPa−1).
  • Report the mean of the acceptable, repeatable values — not the highest.

KCO and VA do not vary in proportion, so dividing does not remove the effect of lung size. Report KCO as its own measurement with its own predicted value, and never present it as a normalised DLCO. Common technical faults

  • Slow inspiration — the most frequent fault. Falsely low DLCO. Coach the snap.
  • Incomplete inspiration — VI below the criterion. Recheck the VC used as the reference, and consider that the patient never emptied to RV first.
  • Valsalva during the hold — raises intrathoracic pressure, lowers pulmonary capillary blood volume, lowers DLCO.
  • Leak during the hold — tracer escapes, VA and DLCO both wrong.
  • Insufficient interval between manoeuvres — residual CO raises the back pressure and lowers each successive result. A stepwise fall across three manoeuvres is this fault until proven otherwise.

Oscillometry (IOS / FOT)

Measured during quiet tidal breathing, so it works for patients who cannot perform a forced manoeuvre — young children, the frail, the severely breathless.

Standard
Technical Standards for Respiratory Oscillometry, ERS 2020 — Eur Respir J 2020;55:1900753
Measures
Respiratory system resistance (R) and reactance (X) across a range of oscillation frequencies
Duration
5–10 minutes
Effort
Tidal breathing only. Effort independent
Order
Perform before spirometry — deep inspirations and forced manoeuvres alter airway tone

How it works

A loudspeaker superimposes small pressure oscillations on the patient's tidal breathing. The device measures the resulting pressure and flow and derives the impedance of the respiratory system, which splits into a resistive component in phase with flow and a reactive component out of phase with it.

Frequency matters because low-frequency oscillations penetrate deep into the lung while high-frequency oscillations are damped in the proximal airways. That is what makes the technique regionally informative.

Procedure & quality

  1. PositionSeated upright, head neutral or slightly extended, nose clip on. Do not let the neck flex — it changes upper airway resistance.
  2. Support the cheeksThe patient's own hands, firmly, on both cheeks and under the chin. Unsupported cheeks act as a shunt and falsely lower resistance. This is the single most important coaching point.
  3. Breathe quietlyRegular tidal breathing, no swallowing, no vocalising, no glottis closure. Recordings of at least 30 seconds.

Bronchodilator response by oscillometry

Reported as a fall in R5. A decrease of around 40 % or more in R5 is widely used as a significant response in adults, and the 2020 standard also supports expressing change in z-scores. The threshold differs from spirometry — do not carry the 10 % rule across.

Core lung function

Respiratory muscle strength

When the VC is low and TLC is low but the lung itself looks normal, the question is usually whether the pump is failing. MIP and MEP answer it non-invasively.

Standards
ATS/ERS Statement on Respiratory Muscle Testing 2002 · ERS Statement on Respiratory Muscle Testing at Rest and During Exercise, 2019 — Eur Respir J 2019;53:1801214
Measures
MIP (PImax), MEP (PEmax), SNIP
Units
cmH2O. MIP is a negative pressure; report the magnitude and state the convention used
Duration
10–15 minutes
Indications
Neuromuscular disease, unexplained restriction or dyspnoea, diaphragm dysfunction, weaning assessment, pre-operative risk

Technique

  1. Choose the interfaceA flanged mouthpiece is standard; a rubber tube-type mouthpiece can be used if the patient cannot seal. The mouthpiece must contain a small leak — a needle-sized orifice roughly 1–2 mm — to prevent glottis closure and to stop the cheek and mouth muscles generating pressure that the chest did not.
  2. MIP from residual volumePatient exhales fully to RV, then makes a maximal inspiratory effort against the occluded mouthpiece. "Blow all the way out … now suck in as hard as you possibly can and keep sucking."
  3. MEP from total lung capacityPatient inhales fully to TLC, then makes a maximal expiratory effort against the occlusion. Support the cheeks with your hands or the patient's. "Full breath in … now blow as hard as you can and keep blowing."
  4. Sustain the effortAt least 1.5 seconds, so that a 1-second average plateau pressure can be taken. Peak pressures alone overstate strength.
  5. RepeatAt least three acceptable efforts, with about a minute of rest between. Continue until the values stop rising — up to five to eight attempts — because there is a learning effect.
  6. Report the highestNot the mean. Values should agree within about 10 % or 20 cmH2O.

SNIP

Sniff nasal inspiratory pressure. A maximal, short, sharp sniff through one unoccluded nostril, with a plug in the other, measured from FRC. It requires no mouthpiece seal, which makes it valuable in facial weakness or bulbar disease, and it complements MIP rather than replacing it. Ten to twenty sniffs; report the highest.

Interpreting a low value

A low MIP has poor specificity — it may reflect true weakness, a poor seal, submaximal effort, or a patient who does not understand the task. A normal value is far more informative than a low one: it effectively excludes clinically important inspiratory muscle weakness. If the value is low, repeat with better coaching before reporting, and say in the comment how well the patient cooperated.

Supine VC is a useful adjunct. A fall of more than about 20–25 % from sitting to supine suggests diaphragm weakness, because the abdominal contents are no longer being supported against a weak diaphragm.

Methacholine challenge

A deliberate, controlled provocation of bronchoconstriction. The highest-risk routine test in the lab, and the one where your safety discipline matters most.

Standard
ERS Technical Standard on Bronchial Challenge Testing: General Considerations and Performance of Methacholine Challenge Tests, 2017 — Eur Respir J 2017;49:1601526
Measures
PD20 — the cumulative delivered dose of methacholine causing a 20 % fall in FEV1

45–90 minutes including recovery
Main value
Its negative predictive value. A normal challenge in a symptomatic patient argues strongly against current asthma

Why PD20 replaced PC20

PC20 is a concentration, so it is only meaningful if everyone uses the same nebuliser output, the same inhalation time and the same breathing pattern. The 2017 standard moved to PD20, the cumulative dose actually delivered in micrograms, which lets any suitable nebuliser or dosimeter be used as long as its delivery characteristics are known and documented.

Before you start

Absolute contraindications

  • FEV1 below 50 % predicted, or below 1.0 L.
  • Myocardial infarction or stroke within 3 months.
  • Uncontrolled hypertension, or known aortic aneurysm.
  • Inability to perform acceptable spirometry.

Relative contraindications — discuss with the physician

  • FEV1 below 60–70 % predicted.
  • Pregnancy or breastfeeding — methacholine is not recommended.
  • Current cholinesterase-inhibitor therapy for myasthenia gravis.
  • Recent respiratory tract infection, within 2–6 weeks, which can cause a transient false positive.
  • Poorly controlled epilepsy, or an inability to cooperate safely.

Withhold before testing

Procedure

  1. Baseline spirometryMust be acceptable and repeatable. Record the baseline FEV1; every subsequent value is judged against it.
  2. Diluent stepAdminister the diluent and repeat spirometry. If FEV1 falls 10 % or more after diluent alone, do not proceed — report it and inform the physician.
  3. Doubling dosesDeliver each methacholine step by the protocol your device is validated for — tidal breathing through a nebuliser for a defined time, or a defined number of dosimeter breaths.
  4. Spirometry after each stepMeasure FEV1 at about 30 and 90 seconds after the end of each inhalation. Use the highest acceptable value. Keep the interval between steps consistent, usually 5 minutes.
  5. Stop at a 20 % fallOr when the maximum protocol dose has been given, or if the patient develops distressing symptoms. Do not chase a bigger fall.
  6. Reverse itAdminister salbutamol 400 µg by spacer. This is mandatory after a positive test and standard practice after any test.
  7. Recover and dischargeRepeat spirometry after 10–15 minutes. Do not discharge until FEV1 has returned to within 10 % of baseline and the patient is symptom-free. If not, repeat the bronchodilator and involve the physician.

Reporting

PD20 is calculated by log-linear interpolation between the last dose producing less than a 20 % fall and the dose producing 20 % or more. Report the cumulative delivered dose in micrograms, the device and protocol used, the baseline FEV1, the maximum percentage fall, and the post-bronchodilator recovery.

Six-minute walk test

Simple equipment, unforgiving standardisation. The distance is only comparable across visits if the corridor, the phrases and the encouragement are identical every time.

Standard
ATS/ERS Technical Standard: Field Walking Tests in Chronic Respiratory Disease, 2014 — Eur Respir J 2014;44:1428–1446
Measures
6-minute walk distance (6MWD), with SpO2, heart rate, and Borg dyspnoea and fatigue scores
Course
A flat, straight, enclosed 30 m corridor with cones at each end and markers every 3 m. Never a treadmill, never a shorter lap
Duration
About 30 minutes including rest and a second test

Contraindications

  • Absolute: unstable angina, or myocardial infarction within the previous month.
  • Relative: resting heart rate above 120 bpm, systolic blood pressure above 180 mmHg, diastolic above 100 mmHg — discuss with the physician before proceeding.

Procedure

  1. Rest firstPatient seated near the start for at least 10 minutes. Record heart rate, blood pressure, SpO2 and Borg dyspnoea and fatigue. Confirm comfortable shoes and usual walking aid.
  2. OxygenIf the patient uses supplemental oxygen, use their prescribed flow and carry the cylinder for them, or have them carry it as they normally would — and record exactly what was done, because it must be repeated identically next time.
  3. Explain and demonstrateWalk one lap yourself at a moderate pace, showing the turn around the cone.
  4. Standard instruction"The object of this test is to walk as far as possible for six minutes. You will walk back and forth in this hallway. Six minutes is a long time to walk, so you will be exerting yourself. You will probably get out of breath or become exhausted. You are permitted to slow down, to stop, and to rest as necessary. You may lean against the wall while resting, but resume walking as soon as you are able. Are you ready to do that? Go."
  5. Do not walk with the patientStand near the start, mark laps, and stay out of their path. Walking alongside sets their pace and invalidates the comparison.
  6. Standard encouragement onlyUse the set phrases at each minute, in an even tone, and nothing else. No "you're doing great", no clapping, no counting down.

You are halfway." 4 min — "Keep up the good work. You have only two minutes left." 5 min — "You are doing well. You have only one minute to go." 15 s left — "In a moment I'm going to tell you to stop. When I do, just stop right where you are and I will come to you." 6 min — "Stop."
  7. At the endMark the spot, record the distance to the nearest metre, and immediately record SpO2, heart rate and post-test Borg scores. Ask what stopped them if they stopped early.
  8. Second testRepeat after at least 30 minutes of rest, on the same day where practical. There is a learning effect of up to about 17 m, so the first test alone underestimates capacity. Report the better of the two distances, and state that two tests were performed.

Chest pain · intolerable dyspnoea · leg cramps · staggering or loss of balance · diaphoresis · a pale or ashen appearance · or a fall in SpO2 below the threshold set in your protocol. Sit the patient down, give oxygen if indicated, monitor, and record the reason and the time on the report.

What to report

  • Distance in metres, and percent of predicted if a reference equation is used — state which one.
  • Lowest SpO2 during the walk and the time to desaturation. A fall of 4 % or more, or any drop below 88 %, is clinically significant and should be highlighted.
  • Whether supplemental oxygen was used, at what flow, and by what device.
  • Pre- and post-test Borg dyspnoea and fatigue, heart rate and blood pressure.
  • Any rest stops: how many, how long, and why.
  • The number of tests performed and which distance is being reported.

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing

The only test that stresses the heart, lungs, circulation and muscle together and lets you see which one gives out first. Resource-heavy, and the most demanding test we run.

A direct measurement of gas exchange and acid–base status, and the reference against which pulse oximetry is judged. Pre-analytical technique decides the accuracy far more often than the analyser does.

In the bronchoscopy suite our role is airway, oxygenation and monitoring. The proceduralist is looking down the scope; someone has to be watching the patient.

Before the procedure

  • Confirm consent, fasting status, allergies, anticoagulation and its last dose, and relevant recent imaging and lung function.
  • Check suction, oxygen supply and delivery devices, the bag-valve-mask, the airway trolley and the resuscitation trolley — all present, connected and working before the patient enters.
  • Confirm reversal agents are available: naloxone for opioids, flumazenil for benzodiazepines.
  • Set up the specimen containers, labels and requests before starting, so nothing is improvised with a sample in hand.
  • Take part in the surgical safety checklist and time-out.

During

  • Deliver supplemental oxygen by nasal cannula or the bronchoscopy mask port, titrated to the target saturation. Anticipate desaturation during suctioning and during lavage.
  • Watch the patient and the monitor, not the video screen. Report desaturation, bradycardia, tachycardia, arrhythmia, hypotension and over-sedation immediately.
  • Manage secretions and support the airway. Have a nasopharyngeal or oropharyngeal airway ready.
  • For EBUS-TBNA, handle needle passes and specimen preparation as agreed with the cytology service, and keep the count of passes per station.
  • Label every specimen with the station or lobe of origin at the moment it is taken. Mislabelling here is a serious patient safety event.

After

  • Monitor in recovery until the patient is alert, protecting their airway, and oxygenating at or near baseline on their usual therapy.
  • Keep the patient nil by mouth until the topical anaesthetic has worn off and the gag reflex has returned — typically about 2 hours.
  • Watch for the recognised complications: bleeding, pneumothorax after transbronchial biopsy, fever, bronchospasm, and hypoxaemia.
  • Give discharge instructions including which symptoms warrant immediate return.
  • Complete the procedure record, the specimen log and the scope reprocessing documentation.

Step zero: is the study good enough to interpret

Before any number is read, check the session grades, the traces, and the technologist's comment. A grade E study with a low FVC may just be a patient who stopped early. Interpretation of an unacceptable study is worse than no interpretation, because it looks authoritative.

The sequence

  1. Check FEV1/FVC against its LLNBelow the LLN (z < −1.645) means obstruction. Use post-bronchodilator values where they exist. Do not use a fixed 0.70 cut-off — it over-diagnoses the elderly and under-diagnoses the young.
  2. Check FVC against its LLNIf the ratio is normal and the FVC is low, the pattern may be restrictive, or it may be a submaximal effort or gas trapping. This is where you need TLC.
  3. Check TLC against its LLNTLC below the LLN confirms restriction. TLC normal with a low FVC and a normal ratio is a non-specific pattern. Obstruction plus a low TLC is a mixed defect.
  4. Check DLCO against its LLNRead it with VA and KCO. A low DLCO with normal mechanics points towards the pulmonary circulation, early interstitial disease, or anaemia.
  5. Grade the severityBy the z-score of FEV1 in obstruction, or of the primary abnormal index otherwise.
  6. Assess bronchodilator responsivenessBy the current 10 %-of-predicted rule.
  7. Compare with previous studiesA change is only meaningful beyond natural within-subject variation. Look at the trend across several visits rather than the last two points.

Two habits that prevent most interpretation errors
  • Never call restriction from spirometry alone. A low FVC with a normal ratio has at least four explanations, and only one of them is a small lung.
  • Never diagnose small airways disease from FEF25–75 alone. Its confidence interval is so wide that isolated abnormality is common in health, and it is heavily dependent on the FVC it is measured over.

Change over time

Serial studies are often more useful than any single report, but only if you compare like with like: the same device where possible, the same reference equation throughout, the same time of day and the same medication state. State on the report which prior study you compared against. A short-term change in FEV1 of less than about 12 % or 200 mL falls within ordinary within-subject variability for most patients, so read the trend rather than the last two points.

Reading the report

Loop patterns

The flow–volume loop carries diagnostic information the numbers do not. Learn these six shapes and you will spot an upper airway lesion before the software flags anything.

Why the upper airway loops look like that

Airways outside the thorax — larynx, extrathoracic trachea — are surrounded by atmospheric pressure. During inspiration the pressure inside them falls below atmospheric, so a floppy extrathoracic lesion is sucked closed and inspiratory flow is limited.

Airways inside the thorax are surrounded by pleural pressure. During forced expiration pleural pressure exceeds airway pressure, so a floppy intrathoracic lesion is squeezed closed and expiratory flow is limited.

A rigid, fixed lesion cannot respond to either pressure, so it limits flow equally in both directions and the loop becomes a rectangle.

The screening ratio

Compare FEF50 with FIF50. In health the ratio is around 1 or below, because inspiratory flows at mid-volume are usually as high as or higher than expiratory. A ratio above about 1 suggests extrathoracic obstruction. Treat it as a prompt to look hard at the loop and to repeat the manoeuvre, not as a diagnosis.

Before you call an upper airway pattern

Repeat it. A flattened inspiratory limb is far more often a submaximal inspiratory effort, a poor mouthpiece seal, or a patient who did not return to TLC. The finding only counts if it is reproducible across several acceptable manoeuvres — and if it is, say so explicitly in your comment so it is not dismissed.

Test order in a combined session

Order matters because each test perturbs the next. Run them like this unless the referral says otherwise.

  1. OscillometryTidal breathing only, so it comes first — deep breaths and forced manoeuvres change airway tone.
  2. Spirometry, pre-bronchodilatorThe reference for everything that follows.
  3. Lung volumesPlethysmography with its linked spirometry.
  4. DLCOAfter lung volumes, since the VC measured there is used to judge the inspired volume.
  5. Respiratory muscle pressuresFatiguing, so keep them late.
  6. Bronchodilator, then post-bronchodilator spirometryLast, because it changes everything measured before it.
